*I recently spent most of a Sunday reorganising my notes into seven neat maps. It felt like the most productive day of the month. I did not produce a thing.*

## A very productive Sunday

The maps are lovely. Every area of my life has a hub now. Every hub links to the right notes. The whole thing hangs together in a way it never did before. I enjoyed every minute.

Then I looked at the drafts folder those maps now organise so beautifully. A dozen half-finished essays, some of them more than a year old. Not one of them was a word closer to done.

The maps made the drafts easier to find. They did nothing to make them easier to write.

**That is the trap. Productivity systems are fascinating, satisfying to build, and very good at feeling like the work while not being the work.**

## What a thing really costs

Every system has a running cost. Learn the tool. Set it up. Decide how to categorise things. Keep the categories tidy. Fix it when it breaks. Resist redesigning it every time you read about a better method.

Most productivity advice ignores that cost entirely. It assumes any improvement in organisation pays for itself.

Cal Newport, in *Digital Minimalism*, borrows a line from Thoreau's *Walden* that I now hold every new tool up against: "The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run." Thoreau meant farmland. It works just as well for a to-do app. The price is not the subscription. It is the hours of your life it quietly asks for.

Larry Tesler, the computer scientist who gave us cut and paste, had a law for this too: every system has a certain amount of complexity that cannot be removed, only moved. Your elaborate setup does not make the complexity of your work disappear. It moves some of it into the setup. Where you now have to look after it.

## The flexibility trap

The apps sell flexibility. Tag anything. Link everything. Build your own workflow. Customise every view.

That sounds like freedom. In practice it means every part of the system is a decision, and every decision is something to second-guess. A paper notebook only lets you write things down in order. That limit quietly deletes hundreds of tiny choices about folders, tags and templates.

The infinitely flexible app offers infinite chances to tweak. **Somewhere along the way you stop being someone who gets things done and become the administrator of your own productivity platform.**

## Resistance has a dashboard now

Steven Pressfield, in *The War of Art*, calls the force that stops you doing important work Resistance. Its favourite trick is to hand you something that looks like work instead.

Reorganising a task list feels productive and lets you dodge the task. Researching a new method feels like progress and delays the thing the method was for. The harder and more important the real work, the more attractive the system becomes. Faced with a difficult essay, an afternoon restructuring my notes gives me all the satisfaction of achievement and none of the strain.

Modern tools give Resistance a dashboard. Streaks, completion rates, an inbox count. You can feel completely in control of a day in which nothing that mattered happened.

## Counting the wrong things

Systems are good at measuring what is easy to count. Tasks ticked. Hours logged. Emails cleared. The most valuable work rarely looks like that.

Thinking hard about a problem ticks nothing off. A good idea can take weeks to form. An essay is invisible until the last day. When the system measures what is countable, it nudges you towards the work that makes numbers, which is usually the busywork.

It is the same mistake I see in AI projects at work: counting what is easy instead of the outcome anyone cares about. Turns out we make it at home too.

## Take things away

The biggest improvements I have made came from removing things, not adding them.

When I [used a dumb phone for a month](/blog/i-used-a-dumb-phone-for-a-month-with-a-modern-twist/), the lesson was not that smartphones are bad. It was that removing options removed decisions, and removing decisions gave me my attention back. Newport recommends almost exactly that as a method: a thirty-day break from every optional technology, then adding back only what earns its place.

The same works on a normal working day. Fixed blocks of time, so you are not negotiating with yourself every morning. One thing at a time, because switching costs more than it feels like. Fewer tools, because each one is another thing to check and update. And a door that is shut for a while, because deep work needs one.

## Small, dumb, durable

There is an old idea from the early days of Unix: write programs that do one thing well and pass plain text between them. It is good advice for personal systems too.

Plain text files any app can open, now and in twenty years. A calendar for time and nothing else. A notebook for thoughts that do not need to be digital. One list of what matters this week. Nothing clever. All of it hard to break, and very little of it needing upkeep.

## Good enough

The most productive people I know have embarrassingly simple systems. Not because they never thought about it, but because they decided the system was not where the value was.

My seven maps are staying. I like them, and they will save me time. But here is the rule I am setting myself: **no improving the system on a day I have not done the work.** Tinkering is the reward, not the replacement.

Your attention is the scarcest thing you have. Be suspicious of anything that promises to protect it while quietly spending it.
