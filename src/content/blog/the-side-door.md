*Every step of my career has felt like finding a door that was not supposed to be there. The side door needs to be visible, or the next person will not know it exists.*

## Two weeks ahead

I left school at 16. It was 2016, and I was supposed to be picking A-levels.

School had never fitted. I did fine in my GCSEs, but the place never suited how I think, and I could not see what two more years of it would give me. So instead of two A-levels I had no real interest in, I went to North West Regional College in Derry and did a BTEC in IT.

Something switched on. For the first time in my life I was ahead of the class. Years later the local paper asked me about it, and I gave them the honest version: **if I wasn't two weeks ahead, I felt two weeks behind.**

Two years later I left with a triple Distinction*, the top grade the course gives. I mention it not to boast. I mention it because at 16 nobody expected it. Including me.

## Eighteen

At 18 everyone hits the same fork. University meant three years, a lot of debt and more of the format that had never suited me. Going straight into work meant a ceiling I could already see.

The door I found was a degree apprenticeship. An employer would hire me, pay me and pay for a degree in Business Technology at Ulster University. Monday to Thursday I was a technologist. Friday I was a student.

It was not the easy option. Full-time work plus a degree is hard, and there was a year in the middle where my marks dipped and I wondered if I had bitten off more than I could chew. But in 2023 I came out with a 2:1, four years of real experience and no student debt.

My dissertation surveyed people across the consulting industry about how AI would change their work. ChatGPT was ten months old. It turned out to be the subject of my career.

## The oldest route there is

We call apprenticeships the alternative route. Historically, they are the original one.

In 1563 the Statute of Artificers made a seven-year apprenticeship the standard way into a skilled trade in England. For most of history, if you wanted to be good at something, you learned it beside someone who already was, getting paid a little and doing real work from day one. The lecture hall was the exception, mostly for the Church, the law and medicine.

Look at who came through the side. Benjamin Franklin started as an apprentice in his brother's print shop at twelve and never went to university. Steve Jobs dropped out of Reed College after a term and stayed on to sit in on classes he actually wanted, including calligraphy. Walter Isaacson's biography traces a line from that class to the typefaces on the first Macintosh.

Somewhere in the last fifty years we flipped it. University became the front door and everything else a side entrance, for people who "didn't get the grades". Schools are judged on how many pupils go to university, so that is where they point everyone.

## Kind and wicked

David Epstein's *Range* gave me words for why my route worked.

He splits fields into kind and wicked. Kind fields have clear rules and instant feedback, like chess or golf, and starting early and narrow pays off. Wicked fields have fuzzy rules and slow feedback, like most careers, and there the people who sample widely and find work that fits them tend to win.

![Two cards comparing kind and wicked fields from David Epstein’s Range. Kind fields like chess and golf have clear rules and instant feedback, so specialising early pays. Wicked fields like most careers and technology have fuzzy rules and slow feedback, so sampling widely and finding a good fit wins.](../../assets/blog/the-side-door/kind-and-wicked.webp)

Technology is about as wicked as it gets. Nobody at 16 knows what the job will look like at 26. I certainly did not. The job I do now barely existed when I picked my course.

I am not against university. It is the right route for plenty of people and plenty of jobs. **My point is narrower. For some people, learning by doing, beside people who are good at it, with real stakes from week one, is simply a better way to learn.** And most of those people never hear the option exists.

## Why it suited my brain

I am dyslexic. I have written about what that does to how I think, and why it suits systems work and AI.

What I did not understand at 16 was how much the *format* of learning matters. School rewards reading fast, memorising and repeating it under exam conditions. Work rewards seeing how a system fits together, noticing what is about to break and fixing it with whatever is to hand. One of those was always going to suit me better.

An apprenticeship let me learn the way my brain works. In context. On real problems. With the theory arriving on Friday to explain what I had been doing all week. The theory stuck because I had already met the thing it described.

## Not a ladder

I used to think of a career as a ladder. One rung after another, in the order everyone else climbs.

Mine has not looked like that. Student, apprentice, live services, AI, and now a technology manager in my own city. Several of those steps did not exist as rungs when I started. They were doors I found, or built, off to the side.

The standard route is a ladder someone else designed. It is a perfectly good ladder. But it is not the building.

## Back to school

Every so often I go back into local schools and colleges to give careers talks and run STEM workshops. I always end up saying some version of the same thing.

You do not have to do A-levels to work in tech. You do not have to go into debt for a degree. If school does not suit the way you think, that might be a fact about school, not about you. And the people who end up building the technology everyone else uses were not all top of the class at 16.

I took the side door. So did a lot of the best engineers I know. It needs to be visible, or the next person will walk straight past it.

If I had heard all that at 15, I would have felt two weeks ahead a lot sooner.
